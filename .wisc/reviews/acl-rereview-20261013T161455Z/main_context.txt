\subsection{Impact of MBR based on Replay and Held-out Validation Gate}

Figure~\ref{fig:mbr_replay_test_ndcg@1_std} shows MBR with bounded replay alone achieved higher final performance on test dataset than the held-out validation gate alone: F1 increased from 0.495 to 0.594 and nDCG@1 from 0.644 to 0.691, relative improvements of 20.0\% and 7.3\%, respectively. Combining MBR with the held-out gate further improved final performance on unseen test tasks, increasing F1 by 3.28\% and nDCG@1 by 4.71\% to 0.724 compared with MBR alone. Relative to each configuration's own initialization, disabling both only increases F1 and nDCG@1 by 21.0\% and 6.8\% which is much lower than the 100.9\% and 38.8\% increase when both are enabled. This supports empirical update selection without implying that unchecked updates invariably worsen performance. With both components enabled, diminishing returns appeared around iteration 5, by which point 96.5\% of the final F1 gain and 93.0\% of the final net nDCG@1 gain had accrued. Later iterations added only 0.011 F1 and 0.014 nDCG@1, indicating an approximate plateau. This suggest diminishing opportunities for useful predicate refinement on the plateau: early iterations correct broad, frequent routing errors, while later iterations encounter harder or less common cases.

\subsection{Performance and Token Usage Tradeoff of MBR Replay and Held-out Gate}

Figure~\ref{fig:performance_token_usage} demonstrates that combining replay and the gate provides the strongest final routing performance, while MBR alone reduces token usage by 10.8\% at the cost of a 7.7\% relative decrease in nDCG@1 compared with using both. Across configurations with and without the gate, replay improves final routing performance by 15.4\% to 23.7\% at the cost of a  40.6\% to 52.9\% increase in token usage.

\subsection{Skill Routing Performance across LLM Backbones}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:routing_performance_across_llms_init}, the cold-start nDCG@1 scores of Qwen3.5 4B, Qwen3.8 27B, and GPT-OSS 120B are 0.520, 0.635, and 0.686, respectively. RouterBSI optimization increases these scores to 0.742, 0.802, and 0.745, corresponding to relative improvements of 42.7\%, 26.3\%, and 8.6\%. Although the relative gain decreases with model size, the final scores are not monotonic: Qwen3.8 27B achieves the highest nDCG@1 among these backbones. The optimized RouterBSI with Qwen3.5 4B approaches GPT-OSS 120B performance, reaching 99.6\% of the GPT-OSS 120B performance. Overall, these results demonstrate that RouterBSI can significantly improve the routing performance of predicates across LLMs. They also suggest that predicates reach a plateau that cannot be overcomed with more capable LLMs, where it's difficult for the predicate to be narrow enough to be false for irrelevant tasks but broad enough to be true for all relevant tasks

\begin{abstract}
% DOCX-P000-END

% DOCX-P001-BEGIN
As agents are expected to solve broader and more complex tasks, they rely on external knowledge saved as skills to handle tasks beyond the LLM's parametric capabilities. As the skill corpora grows into hundreds or thousands of skills, skill routing becomes a bottleneck, where the agent must efficiently retrieve and select the appropriate skills to complete a task. Recent skill routing approaches leverage task feedback to improve skill routing performance online. However, feedback-driven updates to routing criteria can regress performance, and accumulating task feedback at scale requires efficient experience management. We introduce a recursive self-improving algorithm for skill routing in a black-box setting called RouterBSI. It refines task applicability predicates using Minimum Bayes Risk (MBR) with bounded replay to minimize errors on seen tasks and a held-out validation gate to reduce regressions on unseen tasks. The router evaluates predicates for top-k retrieved skills then selects skills if the task satisfies their respective predicate. RouterBSI improves its cold-start top-5 recall of required skills by 24.9\% across different retrievers on tasks from TheoremQA, LogicBench, ToolQA, MedCalc-Bench, CHAMP, and BigCodeBench sharing a 26k skill corpus. On average, it retains 90.5\% of the required skills in top 5 from the top 50 skills retrieved.
% DOCX-P001-END

\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
% DOCX-P075-END

% DOCX-P076-BEGIN
We presented RouterBSI, a non-parametric optmization algorithm for recursive self-improvement that refines explicit skill applicability predicates from routing failures without updating model weights. It combines minimum Bayes risk selection over bounded replay with held-out validation. Experiments on six SRA-Bench datasets show that RouterBSI achieves the strongest average routing performance among evaluated methods and preserves more required skills as the selection budget decreases. Ablations support the complementary contributions of replay and validation and show improvements across model backbones. These findings demonstrate the value of editable predicates and feedback-driven skill routing. Future work can connect skills based on dependencies to improve skill routing performance or jointly optimize predicates and skills to improve downstream performance.
% DOCX-P076-END


=== sections/ethics_statement.tex ===
\section*{Ethical Considerations}

Our experiments reuse existing SRA-Bench tasks, annotations, and skill content, which may contain errors, biases, or harmful instructions. Reuse should respect dataset, skill, and model licenses, while sensitive task data require appropriate access controls and retention policies, particularly when using external model services. Incorrect predicate judgments can omit required skills or admit inappropriate ones, propagating errors to downstream decisions. Bounded replay and held-out validation assess routing behavior rather than factual correctness, fairness, or safety. Results on MedCalc-Bench do not establish clinical validity or suitability for patient care; high-stakes applications require domain-specific validation and qualified human oversight. Retrieved instructions and generated code should be treated as untrusted content, with permission controls and isolated execution before use.

We filter tasks and construct skill-balanced train, validation, and test partitions targeting global proportions of 70\%, 15\%, and 15\%. Every retained gold skill occurs in all three partitions. This design supports evaluating RouterBSI\textquoteright{}s adaptation to held-out tasks involving skills represented during training. Predicate updates require a skill to appear in a training task\textquoteright{}s retrieved top-$k$ pool and incur a routing error; skills never retrieved during training retain their cold-start predicates. \subsection{Implementation Details.}
% DOCX-P057-END

% DOCX-P058-BEGIN
Retrieval uses pretrained embedding models and/or lexical matching without task-split training. Candidate skills are retrieved from the shared external skill corpus in response to the input query. % DOCX-P002-BEGIN
\section{Introduction}
% DOCX-P002-END

% DOCX-P003-BEGIN
Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly rely on reusable skills to extend their capabilities beyond knowledge encoded in model parameters. These skills package procedural knowledge that can be reused across recurring classes of tasks. As skill libraries grow, however, explicitly exposing every available skill in the context becomes increasingly impractical. Recent work therefore shifts toward Skill Retrieval Augmentation (SRA), where skills must be retrieved and loaded from a large-scale skill corpora \citep{su2026skillretrieval} (Figure~\ref{fig:predicate-routing}).
% DOCX-P003-END

% INTEGRATION-BEGIN
\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/routing_figure.png}
\caption{Skill routing with a task applicability predicate. The skill is appropriately selected for task $q_{1}$ because the task asks about the price for a specific trading day. The skill is not accepted for task $q_{2}$ because it asks for the price between a range of years.}
\label{fig:predicate-routing}
\end{figure}
% INTEGRATION-END

% DOCX-P004-BEGIN
In SRA, retrieval based on semantic relevance alone is not enough. Skills must be invoked based on their downstream utility for completing a task to reliably augment the agent's capabilities. Current agents can struggle to distinguish useful skills from irrelevant ones even when the correct skill has been successfully retrieved \citep{su2026skillretrieval}. We refer to skill routing as the process of determining which retrieved candidate skills are applicable to a task and should therefore enter the agent\textquotesingle s active context for completing a task. Reliable routing must distinguish applicability from semantic relatedness: a skill can be topically similar to a task without satisfying the requirements under which its procedure should be invoked. This distinction becomes increasingly important as broader candidate sets introduce irrelevant procedures that can distract the model or interfere with downstream reasoning.
% DOCX-P004-END

% DOCX-P005-BEGIN
Existing routing approaches address this problem through similarity scores, learned utility functions, direct model-based selection, and explicit activation conditions. Learned retrievers and rerankers can improve discrimination among candidates but encode routing criteria implicitly in model parameters and require additional optimization data and compute. In contrast, non-parametric approaches keep the underlying model fixed while modifying external state that influences future decisions. Yet persistent external updates introduce a different reliability problem. An edit motivated by a failure can correct similar failure cases while undoing corrections to previously handled tasks or degrading performance on other cases. As interaction histories grow, retaining all prior examples for checking each update also makes continual adaptation increasingly expensive. This creates a need for a routing mechanism whose applicability criteria are explicit and editable, but whose updates are efficiencly constrained by prior experience and independently checked for generalization. These problems of controlling regressions while bounding used experience serve as the motivation for our approach.
% DOCX-P005-END

% DOCX-P006-BEGIN
We introduce RouterBSI, a recursive self-improving algorithm for skill routing in a black-box setting called RouterBSI. It refines task applicability predicates using Minimum Bayes Risk (MBR) with bounded replay to minimize errors on seen tasks and a held-out validation gate to reduce regressions on unseen tasks. The router evaluates predicates for top-k retrieved skills then selects skills if the task satisfies their respective predicate. RouterBSI improves its cold-start top-5 recall of required skills by an average of 24.9\% across different retrievers on tasks from TheoremQA, LogicBench, ToolQA, MedCalc-Bench, CHAMP, and BigCodeBench sharing a 26k skill corpus. On average, it retains 90.5\% of the required skills in top 5 from the top 50 skills retrieved. Its top-5 recall is 75.5\%, with relative recall improvements over baselines averaging 7.9\% to 33.6\% across retrievers.
% DOCX-P006-END

% DOCX-P007-BEGIN
Our contributions are:
% DOCX-P007-END

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}

% DOCX-P008-BEGIN
\item 
We propose RouterBSI, a non-parametric skill-routing framework that evaluates explicit applicability predicates over retrieved candidates, making routing criteria interpretable and directly editable without updating the underlying language model.
% DOCX-P008-END

% DOCX-P009-BEGIN
\item 
We develop a recursive self-improvement procedure that refines predicates from routing failures, selects updates through minimum Bayes risk over a bounded replay of relevant and irrelevant tasks, and rejects updates with higher held-out validation error than the cold-start predicate.
% DOCX-P009-END

% DOCX-P010-BEGIN
\item 
We evaluate RouterBSI on six SRA-Bench datasets with a shared 26K-skill corpus, achieving the highest aggregate nDCG@10 and Hit@1 among evaluated methods while ablations establish the contributions of replay and validation.
% DOCX-P010-END

\end{itemize}

% DOCX-P073-BEGIN
\section*{Limitations}
% DOCX-P073-END

% DOCX-P074-BEGIN
Our approach has three limitations. First, training is computationally expensive, although optimization converges on the training data and improves overall routing performance. Second, we do not provide a protocol for determining which skills a task requires. The method therefore relies on human annotations or an external labeling mechanism, making optimization dependent on the availability and accuracy of these labels. Third, we keep the predicate checker's instructions fixed, even though errors in interpreting predicates can lead to incorrect skill selections. Our algorithm indirectly improves checking performance by generating and selecting predicate formulations that the checker evaluates more accurately against labeled tasks; however, it does not directly address weaknesses in the checker's instructions.
% DOCX-P074-END

\section{Method}
% DOCX-P022-END

% DOCX-P023-BEGIN
\subsection{Predicate-Based Skill Routing}
% DOCX-P023-END

% DOCX-P025-BEGIN
Skills are selected according to whether the task aligns with the type of task each skill is intended to handle. This is determined by evaluating the skill's predicate statement for the given task. The predicate evaluates to true when the task aligns with the intended task type and to false otherwise.
% DOCX-P025-END

% DOCX-P026-BEGIN
The router first retrieves the top-k skills based on the retrieval score \(r(q,K_{s})\) between task \(q\) and the skill key \(K_{s}\). Given task \(q\), it then evaluates the predicate \(p_{sk}\) of each retrieved skill \(s_{k}\), where \(s_{k}\) ∈ \(S@k\), to determine whether it holds for \(q\): \(p_{sk}(q) = \{ True\ or\ False\}\). The router selects skills whose predicates evaluate to true: \(p_{sk}(q) = True\). The initial cold-start predicates \(p_{si}\) are generated from the skill key \(K_{s}\).
% DOCX-P026-END

% DOCX-P027-BEGIN
\subsection{MBR Update Selection with Bounded Replay}
% DOCX-P027-END

% DOCX-P028-BEGIN
Compare difference between \(P_{q}\)with \(G_{q}\) and identify the any of the following failures: required skill not selected, selected irrelevant skill. Skill \(s_{k}\) is selected only when predicate evaluates to true \(p_{s}\) given the task \(q\): \(p_{sk}(q) = True\). For each skill, we retain up to \(N\) relevant and \(N\) irrelevant training tasks in history buckets \(\mathcal H_s^+\) and \(\mathcal H_s^-\). When repairing a failure on task \(q\), we score candidates on
% DOCX-P028-END

% DOCX-P029-BEGIN
\[\mathcal R_s(q)=\mathcal H_s^+\cup\mathcal H_s^-\cup\{q\},\]
deduplicated by task identifier. The current task is always included; the effective scoring set therefore contains at most \(2N+1\) tasks.
% DOCX-P029-END

% DOCX-P030-BEGIN
For each failed skill \(s_{fk}\) in top \(k\), propose \(M_{p}\) candidate updates to each failed predicate \(p_{f}\) given the task \(q\), skill \(s_{sk}\), and \(q_{rs}\) relevant and \(q_{is}\) irrelevant tasks from previously seen tasks of the failed skill.
% DOCX-P030-END

% DOCX-P031-BEGIN
To reduce overly long and detailed predicates that may not generalize, we limit each proposed candidate \(m_{p} \in M_{p}\) must not exceeds a max length \(l\).
% DOCX-P031-END

% DOCX-P033-BEGIN
Evaluate each candidate and the failed predicate on the replay set.
% DOCX-P033-END

% DOCX-P034-BEGIN
After replay, use MBR to select an update \(m_{p}\) ∈ \(M_{p}\) or the original predicate \(p_{f}\) with the least risk score from the risk function. Our risk function is the average skill selection error on replayed tasks. The selection error is the difference between the expected and predicted relevant/irrelevant label of the skill across tasks where the skill is in the top \(k\):
% DOCX-P034-END

% DOCX-P035-BEGIN
\[\ell(m_p,q,s)=|m_p(q)-\mathds{1}[s\in G_q]|\]
% DOCX-P035-END

% DOCX-P036-BEGIN
\[R_{\mathrm{replay}}(m_p\mid s)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_s|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{R}_s}\ell(m_p,q,s)\]
% DOCX-P036-END

% DOCX-P037-BEGIN
\[m_p^*=\mathop{\arg\min}_{m_p\in M_p\cup\{p_f\}} R_{\mathrm{replay}}(m_p\mid s_{fk})\]
% DOCX-P037-END

% DOCX-P038-BEGIN
\[R_{\mathrm{replay}}(m_p^*\mid s_{fk})\leq R_{\mathrm{replay}}(p_f\mid s_{fk})\]
% DOCX-P038-END

% DOCX-P040-BEGIN
\subsection{Held-Out Validation and Recursive Improvement}
% DOCX-P040-END

% DOCX-P041-BEGIN
Evaluate the modified skill library \(S_{m}\) on a held-out validation set. Then, discard all selected updates \(M_{s}\) of modified skill \(s_{m} \in S_{m}\) with a higher or equal routing error than the initial predicate \(p_{si}\) for the skill \(s_{m}\) on tasks where the skill \(s_{m}\) is included in top \(k\) for task \(t\).
% DOCX-P041-END

% DOCX-P042-BEGIN
\[T_{v,s}=\{q\in T_v\mid s\in S@k\}\]
% DOCX-P042-END

% DOCX-P043-BEGIN
\[\begin{aligned}R_v(m_p\mid s)&=\frac{1}{|T_{v,s}|}\\&\quad\cdot\sum_{q\in T_{v,s}}|m_p(q)-\mathds{1}[s\in G_q]|\end{aligned}\]
% DOCX-P043-END

% DOCX-P044-BEGImathrm{replay}}(N
\[\begin{aligned}&g(T_v,m_p^*,p_{si})\\&\quad=\mathds{1}[R_v(m_p^*\mid s_{fk})< R_v(p_{si}\mid s_{fk})]\end{aligned}\]
% DOCX-P044-END

% DOCX-P045-BEGIN
\[p_{sk}^{\mathrm{(i+1)}}=\begin{cases}m_p^*&\text{if }g(T_v,m_p^*,p_{si})=1;\\p_{sk}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\]

% DOCX-P020-BEGIN
\section{Problem Definition}
% DOCX-P020-END

% DOCX-P021-BEGIN
We study recursive self-improvement for skill routing, where an agent updates skill applicability predicates from routing failures while avoiding regressions. Each skill \(s\) from skill library \(S\) has a predicate \(p_{s}(q) = \{ True\ or\ False\}\) indicating whether it should be selected for task \(q\). A routing failure occurs when this prediction disagrees with whether \(s\) from predicted skills \(P_{q}\) is in ground-truth set of required skills \(G_{q}\), producing either a missing required skill or a selected irrelevant skill. Since \(S\) is a large library of skills, we only evaluate the predicates from skills in the top k based on retrieval score \(r(q,K_{s})\) between task \(q\) and the skill key \(K_{s}\). Given a failure in top \(k\) skills \(s_{k}\), the objective is to iteratively update the affected predicate \(p_{sk}(q)\) to reduce average replay error while using held-out validation to limit regressions..
% DOCX-P021-END

\subsection{Non-Parametric Routing Optimization}
Non-parametric routing optimization updates external routing state from task feedback while keeping model weights fixed. Experience-based methods revise memories and procedures, but revisions can undo earlier improvements \citep{wang2025agentworkflowmemory,ouyang2026reasoningbank,guo2024dsagent}. Structural methods evolve skill relationships, but graph expansions can introduce many irrelevant skills, reducing precision or exceeding context limits \citep{zhang-etal-2025-toolexpnet,bai2026skilldag}. Gated updates limit regressions through surrogate objectives or rollback criteria but don’t directly minimizing routing error on seen tasks \citep{pmlr-v306-mi26d,shi2026programmatic}. Similar to our approach, JTPRO evaluates candidates on sampled training minibatches and held-out validation but does not provide bounded experience management for online adaptation \citep{ghoshal-etal-2026-jtpro}. Retaining earlier routing corrections while bounding experience and evaluation costs remains a challenge. RouterBSI ensures non-increasing empirical error on the retained set while bounding replay evaluation costs. A held-out gate additionally rejects updates that regress validation performance below the cold-start performance.

\subsection{Parametric Routing Optimization}
Parametric routing optimization updates model weights using supervision, preferences, or execution feedback. Learned retrievers and rerankers score relevance or utility of skill for task \citep{qin2024toolllm,zheng2026skillrouter,wang2026skillisnotdocument,li2026skillsinjector,zhou2026mementoskills}, while direct selectors predict which capability to invoke or whether invocation is needed \citep{ICLR2025_b646bdeb,qian-etal-2025-smart,chen2026skillskiplearningselective}. Reinforcement-learning policies optimize skill routing from downstream rewards \citep{wang2026sage,xia2026skillrl,zhang2026memskill,huang-etal-2026-toolomni,wu2026maestro}. These approaches require large training data and encode routing criteria implicitly in model weights. RouterBSI keeps the model fixed and refines natural-language applicability predicates that humans can inspect and edit.

\subsection{Static Routing Without Feedback-driven Optimization}
Static routing uses retrieval or inference-time selection without persistently optimizing routing criteria from task feedback. Similarity methods retrieve skills through semantic or lexical matching \citep{wang2024voyager,xiao2026skillsightcalibratinggenericcontent,su2026skillretrieval}, while structured methods organize search through predefined hierarchies or relationships \citep{li2026agentskillos,sun2026corpus2skill,wang2026graphskill,packer2023memgpt,liang2026skillnetcreateevaluateconnect,liu2025toolplanner}. LLM-based methods select or compose capabilities at inference time \citep{chen2026cuaskill,xia2026grasp,NEURIPS2023_77c33e6a,NEURIPS2024_e41efb03}. These approaches support skill routing but do not convert failures into persistent corrections. RouterBSI uses routing failures to refine applicability predicates online in a black-box setting.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Skill Routing Performance}

RouterBSI improves routing performance of base retrievers by an average nDCG@1 and nDCG@10 across retrievers of 67.0\% and 39.7\%, respectively Table~\ref{tab:skill_routing_performance_average}. SkillRouter is the strongest routing baseline, yet RouterBSI the nDCG@1 and nDCG@10 is 10.9\% and 8.4\% higher on average. Compared to the other non-parametric optimization baselines, RouterBSI has a mean 29.2\% and 26.7\% nDCG@10 score over SkillDAG and Skill-Pro, respectively.

The exception is MedCalc-Bench and TheoremQA, where RouterBSI underperforms SkillRouter by a mean of 5.01\% and 6.10\% respectively. RouterBSI could further improve its performance on these cases with more training iterations or relying on a human to directly edit the predicates.

\subsection{Required Skill Retention}

RouterBSI retains more required skills from initial top 50 retrieval than other baselines as we reduce the max k of skills loaded into LLM Agent. For instance, Figure~\ref{fig:average_recall_degradation} shows that RouterBSI retained 90.5\% of the required skills in top 5 from the top 50 skills retrieved, whereas the retention of baselines ranged from 59.5\% to 83.9\% on average across retrievers. Despite its low retention in top 5 of 59.5\%, SkillDAG has an average  16.1\% more recall of required skills in top 50 than the base retrievers with an average recall score of 96.86\% compared to 83.45\%, respectively. The higher coverage in top 50 of SkillDAG’s expansion plus the retrieval score can be a better alternative for initial retrieval than relying on the retrievers alone. Combining the higher coverage of SkillDAG in top 50 with better retention of RouterBSI could lead to better routing performance.

\subsection{Downstream Performance}

Figure~\ref{fig:downstream_average} demonstrates that combining RouterBSI selected skills with some additional retrieved skills leads to slightly higher mean downstream performance as $\mathrm{RouterBSI}_{\mathrm{top}5}$’s average performance across retrievers is 2.5\% higher than $\mathrm{RouterBSI}_{\mathrm{strict}}$. $\mathrm{RouterBSI}_{\mathrm{top}5}$’s average success rate across retrievers reaches 95.7\% of the oracle performance which is 1.5\% to 20.5\% higher downstream performance than other baselines except SkillDAG. SkilDAG reaches 99.0\% of oracle success rate despite having similar recall of required skills as $\mathrm{RouterBSI}_{\mathrm{top}5}$ with only a 4.41\% difference. This finding suggests expanding selected skills to include dependent skills could improve downstream performance even if the dependent skills are not required. Overall, results show that the higher RouterBSI retention of required skill in top 5 translates to a better average routing performance.
